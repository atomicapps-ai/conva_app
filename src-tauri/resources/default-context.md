# General conversation

## Overview
The baseline briefing Ally grounds in when no call-specific context is active — active-listening habits, a small cross-domain glossary, and general good practices that apply to almost any professional conversation: interviews, meetings, sales calls, and everything between. v1, hand-authored; a future version grows this from real usage (community voting + LLM inference).

## Active listening & response habits
- Reflect back what you heard before responding — "So what I'm hearing is…" — confirms understanding and buys a beat to think.
- Ask one clarifying question rather than guessing at intent.
- Name the tradeoff, don't just state a position — "I'd lean toward X because Y, though Z is a real cost."
- A 2–3 second pause reads as thoughtful, not unprepared — silence is fine.
- Summarize the decision or next step out loud before moving on, so both sides leave aligned.

## Glossary
- **Stakeholder** — anyone with a material interest in the outcome, not just the decision-maker.
- **Scope creep** — a project's requirements silently growing beyond what was originally agreed.
- **ROI** — return on investment; the value gained relative to what was spent.
- **SLA** — a service-level agreement; a committed standard of performance, usually with a penalty for missing it.
- **KPI** — a key performance indicator; the specific number used to judge success.
- **Runway** — how long current resources (usually cash) last at the current burn rate.
- **North star** — the single goal every other decision gets weighed against.

## Good practices
- Lead with the answer, then the reasoning — most listeners want the conclusion first.
- Quantify claims when possible ("cut latency 40%" beats "made it faster").
- Flag assumptions explicitly ("assuming budget stays flat…") so they can be challenged.
- Close a request with a specific ask, not an open question — "Can you approve X by Friday?" beats "What do you think?"

## Watch-outs
- Hedging ("um, I think, maybe") reads as uncertain even when the underlying answer is solid — don't fill silence with it.
- Absolute claims ("never," "always") invite a single counterexample to undercut the whole point.
